The number a home-search app tells you that you can "afford" and the number that actually fits your life are rarely the same. Here's how lenders really decide — and how to find a figure you'll still feel good about after you've moved in.

It's tempting to start house hunting by typing a price into a search filter. But affordability isn't a single sticker price — it's a monthly payment you can carry comfortably, built from several moving parts. Understanding those parts puts you back in control of the conversation.

Lenders think in monthly payments, not purchase prices

When we evaluate how much you can borrow, we're really asking one question: can this monthly payment fit reasonably within your income alongside your other obligations? The tool we use to answer it is your debt-to-income ratio, or DTI.

DTI compares your total monthly debt payments — your future mortgage, plus car loans, student loans, credit-card minimums, and similar — against your gross monthly income. A common guideline many loan programs lean on is sometimes described as the "28/36" rule: roughly 28% of gross income toward the housing payment, and up to about 36% toward total debt. These are general reference points, not hard limits — different programs allow different ranges, and strong credit or reserves can change the picture.

Your real budget is the payment you can live with — not the maximum a calculator will let you borrow.

The payment is more than principal and interest

This is where many first-time buyers get surprised. Your monthly housing payment usually includes four things, often abbreviated PITI:

  • Principal & Interest — paying down the loan plus the cost of borrowing.
  • Property Taxes — set by your local jurisdiction and collected monthly into escrow.
  • Homeowners Insurance — also typically collected monthly into escrow.
  • Mortgage Insurance — if your down payment is under 20% on many loan types, expect PMI (or an equivalent) until you build enough equity.

On top of PITI, don't forget HOA dues if the property has them, and a realistic line for maintenance — a common rule of thumb is to set aside roughly 1% of the home's value per year for upkeep.

What we actually look at

Beyond DTI, three factors shape the loan you'll qualify for and the terms you're offered:

  • Credit history. Your score and track record influence the programs available to you and the rate you're offered.
  • Down payment. More money down lowers your loan amount and can remove mortgage insurance — but a smaller down payment isn't a dealbreaker; several programs are built for 3–5% down, and some for $0 down.
  • Cash reserves. Money left in the bank after closing reassures lenders you can weather a surprise.

A smarter way to start

Instead of guessing, run a quick scenario through our mortgage calculator to see how price, down payment, and rate move your payment. Then, when you're ready for a real number, a pre-approval translates all of this into a concrete figure you can shop with confidently — and it's free, with no obligation.

You open your mail, and your servicer says your monthly mortgage payment is going up — sometimes by a noticeable amount. Before you panic: an escrow shortage is common, it's usually not an error, and you have options.

First, what is escrow?

When you have an escrow account (most homeowners do), your monthly payment includes more than your loan. A portion is set aside each month to cover your property taxes and homeowners insurance. Your servicer holds that money and pays those bills for you when they come due, so you don't have to save up for a large tax bill on your own.

Why a shortage happens

Once a year, your servicer reviews the account — this is the annual escrow analysis. A shortage simply means the bills they paid on your behalf cost more than what was collected. The usual culprits:

  • Property taxes went up. Reassessments and local rate changes are the most common cause — and they're outside your lender's control.
  • Your insurance premium increased. Premiums have risen in many areas; a higher premium means more needs to be collected.
  • A first-year estimate caught up to reality. On newer loans, the initial escrow figures were estimates; the first analysis trues them up.
A shortage isn't a penalty or a mistake — it's the account catching up to what your taxes and insurance actually cost.

What your notice is really telling you

The statement typically shows two things working together: the shortage amount (what the account came up short over the past year) and a new monthly payment going forward (which now collects enough to cover the higher bills and rebuild the cushion). That's why the increase can feel larger than the tax or insurance change alone.

Your options

Most servicers give you two ways to handle the shortage:

  • Spread it over 12 months. The shortage is divided across your next year of payments. Easiest on cash flow, but your monthly payment rises more.
  • Pay the shortage in a lump sum. You clear the deficit at once, which keeps your ongoing payment increase smaller (it still rises to cover the higher future bills, just not the past shortfall).

How to keep it from stinging next year

  • Read your annual escrow analysis when it arrives instead of setting it aside.
  • Shop your homeowners insurance periodically — a better premium directly lowers your escrow.
  • Review your property tax assessment. If it looks too high, many counties have an appeal process.
  • Ask about refinancing only if the broader numbers make sense — escrow changes alone usually aren't a reason to refinance.

If you're self-employed, you already know the catch: the write-offs that lower your tax bill can also make your income look smaller on paper. A bank statement loan is built to see the income those tax returns hide.

The self-employed lending problem

Traditional mortgages verify income with tax returns and W-2s. That works cleanly for salaried borrowers — but business owners, contractors, and freelancers legitimately deduct expenses to reduce taxable income. The result is a tax return that understates the actual cash flowing through the business. A strong earner can look, on paper, like they barely qualify.

What a bank statement loan does differently

Instead of leaning on tax returns, a bank statement loan qualifies you based on the deposits into your bank accounts over a recent period — typically the last 12 or 24 months. The lender reviews those statements to estimate your real, ongoing income.

It's designed for people whose tax returns don't tell the whole story — not as a shortcut around documentation.

How qualification generally works

  • Statement period. You provide 12–24 months of personal and/or business bank statements.
  • Deposit analysis. The lender totals qualifying deposits and applies an expense factor to estimate net income — business accounts are often adjusted to account for overhead.
  • Time in business. Expect to show a track record, commonly around two years of self-employment.
  • Credit & down payment. As with any loan, your credit profile and down payment shape your options and terms.

What to have ready

Gathering these in advance makes the process noticeably smoother:

  • Consecutive bank statements for the full period (no gaps).
  • Proof of self-employment — a business license, CPA letter, or similar.
  • Clarity on which account your income lands in, and whether it's personal or business.

The honest trade-offs

Bank statement loans open doors, but they're a specialized product. Because they don't use traditional income documentation, they often carry different rate and down-payment requirements than a conventional loan. For many self-employed buyers, that trade is well worth the ability to qualify on their true income — but it's worth understanding up front.

Let's see if it fits

Every self-employed situation is different — sole proprietors, S-corps, and 1099 contractors each look a little different to a lender. The fastest way to know what you qualify for is a short conversation. Buying your first home is equal parts exciting and overwhelming. This playbook walks you through the whole journey in plain English — from getting your finances ready to picking up the keys — so nothing catches you off guard.

1. Start with your number, not a house

It's tempting to start scrolling listings, but the first real step is understanding what you can comfortably afford. Your monthly payment — not the purchase price — is what shapes your daily life. Before anything else, get a pre-approval: it's free, doesn't obligate you, and turns a vague budget into a concrete number you can shop with.

A pre-approval also makes your offer far stronger. In a competitive market, sellers take pre-approved buyers seriously and often won't consider offers without one.

2. Get your finances buyer-ready

A few months of preparation can meaningfully improve your options. Focus on:

  • Credit. Pull your reports, dispute errors, and avoid opening or closing accounts before applying. Pay down balances where you can — lower utilization helps.
  • Savings. You'll need money for a down payment, closing costs, and a cushion of reserves. Lenders like to see you'll still have savings after closing.
  • Stability. Avoid changing jobs or making large, unexplained deposits right before or during the process — both can complicate underwriting.

3. Understand the real costs

Your upfront cost is more than the down payment. Plan for three buckets:

  • Down payment. Many programs allow far less than 20% — some as low as 3–5%, and certain programs require nothing down. A smaller down payment usually means mortgage insurance until you build equity.
  • Closing costs. Typically a few percent of the loan amount — lender fees, title, appraisal, taxes, and prepaid escrow items.
  • Reserves. Money left over after closing for moving, repairs, and life.
The goal isn't to borrow the maximum you qualify for — it's to buy a home that still feels comfortable on a slow month.

4. The buying timeline

  1. Pre-approval — verify your numbers and get your letter.
  2. House hunt — shop within your approved range with an agent you trust.
  3. Make an offer — your agent and loan officer help you structure it.
  4. Under contract — inspection, appraisal, and underwriting happen here. We handle the heavy lifting.
  5. Final approval & closing — sign, fund, and get your keys.

5. Mistakes first-timers make (and how to skip them)

  • Shopping for homes before getting pre-approved.
  • Forgetting closing costs and reserves in the budget.
  • Making big purchases (a car!) or opening credit during the process.
  • Skipping the inspection to win a bid.
  • Not asking questions — there's no such thing as a dumb one.

Mortgage rates can feel mysterious — they move daily, everyone has an opinion, and the headlines rarely match your quote. Here's what actually drives your rate, and the parts you can control.

What a mortgage rate actually is

Your rate is the cost of borrowing, expressed as a yearly percentage. Even a small difference changes your monthly payment and the total interest you'll pay over the life of the loan — which is why it's worth understanding rather than guessing.

What moves rates (that you don't control)

Mortgage rates are tied to broad market forces, not set by any single lender. In general, they're influenced by:

  • The bond market. Mortgage rates tend to track the yields on certain bonds; when those move, rates follow.
  • Inflation and the economy. Rates generally respond to inflation trends and overall economic conditions.
  • Federal Reserve policy. The Fed doesn't set mortgage rates directly, but its decisions ripple through the markets that do.

The takeaway: no lender can promise where rates are headed, and anyone who does should give you pause.

What you do control

This is where the real opportunity is. Several personal factors shape the rate you're offered:

  • Credit profile. A stronger credit history generally earns a better rate.
  • Down payment / equity. More skin in the game can improve your terms.
  • Loan type and term. Different programs and a 15- vs. 30-year term price differently.
  • Discount points. You can sometimes pay an upfront fee to "buy down" your rate — worth it only if you'll keep the loan long enough to break even.
  • Rate lock. Once you're under contract, locking protects your rate for a set window while you close.
You can't time the market — but you can show up as the strongest borrower you can be.

Rate vs. APR — what's the difference?

Your rate is the cost of borrowing the principal. The APR folds in certain fees to reflect the broader yearly cost, which is why it's usually a bit higher. Comparing APRs can help you weigh offers, but always look at the full picture with your loan officer.

Should you wait for rates to drop?

Trying to time the bottom is a gamble even the experts lose. The better questions are: does this home and payment fit your life now, and what's your plan if rates change later? If a future drop makes sense, refinancing is always an option down the road. Marry the house, date the rate — as the saying goes.

The bottom line

Focus your energy on the levers you control and lean on a loan officer to time your lock and choose the right program. That's how you get the best rate available to you — regardless of the headlines.

Refinancing only makes sense if you stay in the home long enough to recoup what it costs. This worksheet helps you find your exact break-even point in about five minutes. Print it and fill it in.

The idea in one sentence

A refinance has upfront costs but lowers your monthly payment. Your break-even month is when your accumulated monthly savings finally cover those upfront costs. Stay past it, and you're ahead.

The formula

Break-even (months) = Total refinance costs ÷ Monthly savings

Fill in your numbers

LineYour number
A. Current monthly payment (P&I)$ ______
B. New monthly payment (P&I) after refi$ ______
C. Monthly savings (A − B)$ ______
D. Total refinance costs (all fees)$ ______
E. Break-even months (D ÷ C)______ months

A worked example

Say your current payment is $2,400 and your new payment would be $2,150 — that's $250 in monthly savings (line C). If the refinance costs $6,000 (line D), then:

$6,000 ÷ $250 = 24 months. You'd break even in two years. Plan to stay longer than that, and the refinance likely makes sense.

Beyond the math

The worksheet covers payment savings, but a few things don't fit neatly in a formula:

  • Loan term reset. Stretching back to a new 30-year term lowers the payment but can increase total interest. Compare lifetime cost, not just the monthly number.
  • Goal beyond savings. Some refinances aren't about lowering the payment — they remove mortgage insurance, tap equity, or move from an adjustable to a fixed rate.
  • How you pay costs. Rolling costs into the loan changes the math versus paying out of pocket.

Self-employed borrowers have more options than ever — but the smoothest closings start with the right paperwork. Gather these once and you'll move through underwriting far faster, whether you go conventional or bank-statement.

Identity & basics

  • Government-issued photo ID
  • Social Security number / tax ID
  • Current address history (typically 2 years)

Proof of self-employment

  • Business license or registration
  • A CPA or licensed tax preparer letter (if available)
  • Website or professional listing that confirms your business

Income documentation

What you'll need depends on the loan type — your loan officer will tell you which path fits:

  • Bank statement loan: 12–24 months of consecutive bank statements (personal and/or business — no gaps)
  • Conventional: 2 years of personal and business tax returns, with all schedules
  • Year-to-date profit & loss statement
  • 1099s, if you receive them

Assets & reserves

  • Recent statements for checking, savings, and investment accounts
  • Documentation for the source of your down payment
  • Proof of reserves left after closing

Property (once you're under contract)

  • Purchase agreement
  • Homeowners insurance quote
  • Most recent mortgage statement (if refinancing)
Consistency is everything: statements with no gaps, deposits you can explain, and one clear account where your income lands.

A few pro tips

  • Keep business and personal banking separate — it makes deposit analysis far cleaner.
  • Avoid large transfers between accounts right before applying; they raise questions.
  • If a deposit is unusual (a tax refund, a gift, a one-time sale), keep a note explaining it.

If you've served, the VA loan is one of the most powerful benefits you've earned — and one of the most misunderstood. Here's a clear, no-jargon start so you can use it with confidence.

Who's eligible

Eligibility generally extends to qualifying veterans, active-duty service members, certain members of the National Guard and Reserves, and some surviving spouses. Eligibility is confirmed with a Certificate of Eligibility (COE) — we can help you request it.

Why it's such a strong benefit

  • $0 down payment on eligible purchases, up to your entitlement — a rare and valuable feature.
  • No monthly mortgage insurance, unlike many low-down-payment loans, which can meaningfully lower your payment.
  • Competitive rates and flexible qualifying guidelines.
  • Limits on certain closing costs the VA won't let you be charged.

Understanding the funding fee

Most VA loans include a one-time VA funding fee that helps keep the program running for future borrowers. The amount varies based on factors like your down payment and whether it's your first use. It can usually be rolled into the loan rather than paid out of pocket.

Importantly, some borrowers — for example, those receiving compensation for a service-connected disability — may be exempt from the funding fee. We'll help you find out where you stand.

The VA loan isn't a one-time benefit — many borrowers use it more than once over a lifetime.

Using it more than once

Your VA entitlement can often be restored and reused — for instance, after you sell a home and pay off the prior VA loan. In some cases borrowers even have more than one VA loan at a time. The rules around entitlement are specific, so it's worth a conversation to map out your situation.
